Yorkie Weight Chart by Age — and Why Popular Charts Disagree

Three widely shared Yorkie growth charts give an 8-week-old puppy anywhere from 10 ounces to 2.5 pounds. Here is what the breed standard and the published growth research actually say, a realistic age-by-age reference, and two small tools to check your own puppy's trend.

A Yorkshire Terrier weight chart is useful for one thing: spotting when a puppy's growth stops following a normal path. It is far less useful as a target. The official AKC breed standard for the Yorkshire Terrier sets only a ceiling — weight must not exceed seven pounds — and gives no minimum and no age-by-age targets. Every week-by-week Yorkie chart you find online was built from breeder records or owner surveys, not from a published study of Yorkie puppies. That is why they disagree, and why your puppy can be healthy while sitting "off the chart".

The quick answer: typical Yorkie weight by age

The table below combines the ranges published by several popular Yorkie growth charts. Treat the overlap as the "typical" zone, not as a pass/fail line. Adult Yorkies most commonly land between 4 and 7 lb, and smaller adults of 3 lb or so exist, usually with more fragile health.

Notice how wide the 8-week row is. That is not sloppiness: it reflects the real spread between a puppy who will mature at 3 lb and one who will mature at 7 lb. A 3 lb adult and a 7 lb adult are both Yorkies, and at 8 weeks one of them can weigh more than twice as much as the other.

Two charts publish a single number per age, which hides the normal variation completely; a puppy heading for a 6 lb adulthood will look "overweight" on them from the first week. The one chart that gives ranges is much closer to what owners report. If a chart shows one number per age, read it as the middle of the road, nothing more.

What the published research says about toy-breed growth

There is no large published weight-by-age dataset just for Yorkies, but there is good research on small dogs in general and one controlled Yorkie study:

  • Small breeds finish growing early. A comparison of growth curves across 12 breeds (Hawthorne et al., Journal of Nutrition, 2004 — summary) found the rapid growth phase in small breeds ends at about 3 months, while large and giant breeds keep growing fast for one to two more months.
  • Very small dogs reach maturity at 8–12 months. The largest growth study to date built standard growth charts from records of more than 6 million young dogs seen at US primary-care vet hospitals (Salt et al., PLOS ONE, 2017). Yorkshire Terriers fall into its smallest category, dogs with an adult weight under 6.5 kg.
  • Yorkie puppies need less energy than generic formulas predict. A study that followed 22 Yorkshire Terrier puppies from 10 to 52 weeks (Alexander, Colyer & Morris, Journal of Nutritional Science, 2017) measured the energy needed to keep them at ideal body condition. Needs fell from about 810 kJ (≈194 kcal) per kg0.75 per day at 10 weeks to about 500 kJ (≈120 kcal) per kg0.75 per day at one year. The standard NRC equation overestimated their needs between 10 and 20 weeks.

That last finding matters in practice. If you work out food portions from a generic puppy formula, a growing Yorkie can easily be overfed, and small and toy breeds are already prone to becoming overweight. Weigh your puppy and adjust portions to its weight and body condition, rather than relying only on the bag's feeding table.

How to use a weight chart properly: watch the curve, not the number

The Salt et al. growth standards are read the same way as children's growth charts. A puppy is expected to follow roughly the same centile line as it grows. The warning sign is crossing centile lines. Moving up two or more lines was associated with dogs becoming overweight by early adulthood; moving down was associated with underweight dogs or diseases that slow growth. For a Yorkie owner, that means:

  1. Weigh weekly until 6 months, then monthly. Use a kitchen or baby scale that reads in grams; a bathroom scale cannot detect the 30–50 g changes that matter in a 1 kg puppy.
  2. Weigh at the same time of day, ideally before the morning meal, so one feeding does not distort the reading.
  3. Plot the numbers. A steady upward line is the goal, whatever height it starts at.
  4. Treat any weight loss in a puppy as significant. In a toy-breed puppy, a flat or falling week can be the first sign of illness, parasites or simply not eating enough. Under 4 months it also overlaps with the risk window described in our guide to Yorkie puppy hypoglycemia.

Estimating adult weight from puppy weight

Owners usually want one thing from a chart: how big will this puppy get? Breeders use two rules of thumb, repeated on most Yorkie growth pages: adult weight ≈ 8-week weight × 3, or adult weight ≈ 12-week weight × 2. Neither has been tested in a published study. YorkieInfoCenter, whose prediction tables come from owner surveys, itself warns of a 0.5–1 lb margin. The tool below shows both rules side by side so you can see how far apart they can be.

Parents matter more than any formula. If both parents are around 5 lb, a 7 lb adult is unlikely whatever the 8-week weight. Ask the breeder for the parents' adult weights, and for the puppy's weight log from birth.

Adult Yorkies: body condition matters more than the scale

Once a Yorkie stops growing, usually between 8 and 12 months, the question changes from "is it growing?" to "is it lean?". A 6.5 lb Yorkie with a large frame can be ideal, while a 4.5 lb Yorkie with a small frame can be overweight. Vets use the 9-point body condition score shown on the WSAVA body condition chart, where 4–5 out of 9 is ideal. The validation work behind the scale (Laflamme, 1997) linked each point to roughly 5% body fat. You will often see "each point equals 10% overweight" online, but that conversion is not on the WSAVA chart.

The Yorkie coat makes visual scoring unreliable. Use your hands instead:

  • Ribs: you should feel them easily under a thin fat layer without pressing, like the back of your hand.
  • Waist: seen from above, after wetting the coat or at bath time, there should be a visible narrowing behind the ribs.
  • Tummy tuck: from the side, the belly should rise towards the hind legs rather than hang level.

Bath time is the easiest moment to do this check, because a wet coat shows the real outline; our guide on how often to bathe a Yorkie covers the routine. Weight and mouth health are also linked in toy breeds: a Yorkie that suddenly loses weight or eats more slowly may have painful teeth, which is common in the breed, as our Yorkshire Terrier dental disease statistics show.

"Teacup" Yorkies and puppies far below the chart

"Teacup", "micro" and "pocket" are sales labels, not sizes. No kennel club recognises them; the AKC standard simply sets a 7 lb maximum. A puppy marketed as a teacup is just a very small Yorkie, and very small toy puppies have less margin for error. They have smaller glycogen reserves, so a missed meal can cause low blood sugar quickly, and they can be easily injured. If your puppy sits well below the lowest row of the chart at 12 weeks, it is not a reason to panic, but it is a reason to keep meals frequent and to tell your vet. Persistent low blood sugar or poor growth in a Yorkie should prompt a check for a portosystemic (liver) shunt, a condition the breed is predisposed to.

When to call the vet about your Yorkie's weight

  • A puppy under 6 months loses weight, or does not gain for two consecutive weekly weigh-ins.
  • Weight loss comes with lethargy, trembling, vomiting, diarrhoea or refusing food (urgent under 4 months).
  • An adult loses more than about 10% of its body weight without a planned diet. For a 5 lb Yorkie that is only half a pound.
  • An adult keeps gaining, or scores 6/9 or higher on the rib-and-waist check.

Frequently asked questions about Yorkie weight

How much should a Yorkie weigh at 8 weeks?

Most published charts put an 8-week-old Yorkie somewhere between about 0.6 and 2.5 lb (280–1,130 g). The range is wide because puppies heading for 3 lb and 7 lb adult weights are already very different at 8 weeks. Steady weekly gain matters more than the exact number.

At what age is a Yorkie fully grown?

Most Yorkies reach adult weight between 8 and 12 months. Research on small breeds shows the fastest growth ends at around 3 months, after which weight gain slows steadily. Some dogs fill out slightly until about 15 months.

What is the ideal adult Yorkie weight?

The AKC breed standard only says a Yorkshire Terrier must not exceed 7 lb. Most adults weigh 4–7 lb. The right weight for an individual dog is the one at which it has a body condition score of 4–5 out of 9: ribs easy to feel, visible waist.

Can I predict my Yorkie puppy's adult weight?

Roughly. Breeders multiply the 8-week weight by 3 or the 12-week weight by 2. These rules have not been validated in published studies and typically miss by 0.5–1 lb. The parents' adult weights are often a better guide.

Is my Yorkie overweight if it weighs more than 7 lb?

Not necessarily. The 7 lb limit is a conformation standard for show dogs, not a health threshold. A larger-framed Yorkie can be lean at 8 lb. Use the rib, waist and tummy-tuck check and ask your vet for a body condition score.

8 in 10 Yorkshire Terriers Have Dental Disease—Here's Why

The AVMA estimates 80% of dogs over three have periodontal disease. For Yorkshire Terriers — with their small jaws, crowded teeth, and tendency to retain baby teeth — the risk starts earlier and progresses faster. Here is what the data says and what actually prevents it.

The headline statistic and why Yorkies are more exposed than average

The American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) states that periodontal disease is the most common clinical condition in adult dogs and cats, with an estimated 80% of dogs over the age of 3 showing some degree of the disease. That figure alone is striking. For Yorkshire Terriers, the breed-specific anatomy raises the baseline risk further.

A Yorkshire Terrier's adult weight ranges from 2 to 3.2 kg (up to 7 lb) [AKC breed standard] — one of the smallest jaw sizes of any recognised breed. Adult teeth that would fit comfortably in a medium-sized dog's mouth are packed into a significantly smaller space. Crowded teeth trap more plaque, are harder to clean mechanically, and provide more surface area for tartar to accumulate between contact points.

The four stages of periodontal disease

The American Veterinary Dental College (AVDC) defines four stages of periodontal disease in dogs. Understanding which stage a dog is at determines what treatment is possible and what has already been lost:

Stage 1 — Gingivitis (reversible)

Plaque has caused inflammation of the gums but the attachment between tooth and bone is intact. The gum line is red and may bleed when probed. This is the only stage where full reversal is possible — a professional cleaning followed by consistent daily brushing can return the gum tissue to health. Most Yorkies that receive annual professional cleanings and daily home brushing stay at Stage 1 or never progress beyond it.

Stage 2 — Early periodontitis (up to 25% attachment loss)

The connective tissue and bone anchoring the tooth have begun to break down. The pocket between gum and tooth deepens. Professional cleaning can halt progression but cannot reverse bone already lost. VCA Animal Hospitals notes that most dogs presenting for their first adult dental cleaning under general anaesthesia are already at Stage 2 — meaning they have irreversible bone loss that could have been avoided with earlier intervention.

Stage 3 — Moderate periodontitis (25–50% attachment loss)

Significant bone loss. Teeth may begin to loosen. Pain is likely, though dogs are expert at hiding dental pain and continuing to eat normally. The AVDC recommends a case-by-case assessment at this stage — some teeth can be saved with advanced periodontal therapy, others require extraction.

Stage 4 — Advanced periodontitis (over 50% attachment loss)

Severe bone loss, significant tooth mobility, active infection. Extraction is the standard of care at this stage. Bacteria from Stage 4 disease are actively entering the bloodstream. The AVMA explicitly notes the documented links between severe periodontal disease and damage to the heart valves, kidneys, and liver.

Why Yorkies are structurally predisposed

Beyond the small jaw size, two breed-specific traits put Yorkshire Terriers at additional risk compared to even other toy breeds:

Retained deciduous teeth

A retained deciduous tooth is a baby tooth that fails to fall out when the adult tooth erupts. Instead of the baby tooth loosening as the adult tooth pushes through, both teeth occupy the same socket simultaneously — creating a double row. Toy breeds including Yorkies are particularly prone to this. The result is a pocket between the two teeth that traps food and bacteria continuously, accelerating tartar and pocket formation at that site.

Retained deciduous teeth are typically identified at the puppy's first dental examination and extracted while the puppy is already under anaesthesia for spay or neuter. If a Yorkie has already had a neuter or spay without retained tooth assessment, a dedicated dental examination under short anaesthesia is worthwhile before the first birthday. VCA Animal Hospitals covers the extraction protocol in detail.

Tooth-to-jaw-size ratio

A Yorkie's 42 adult teeth are proportionally much larger relative to jaw length than the same teeth in a medium or large breed. Contact points between adjacent teeth are tighter, interproximal spaces are narrower, and the tongue has less room to provide natural mechanical cleaning. This is why plaque builds up in Yorkies even in dogs whose owners are attentive. The anatomy works against the owner in ways that require more frequent professional cleaning — typically annually rather than every 2–3 years as might be adequate for a larger breed.

Systemic consequences: beyond the mouth

Untreated periodontal disease does not stay localised in the mouth. The AVMA documents the pathway: bacteria in deep periodontal pockets enter the bloodstream through inflamed gum tissue, travel to organs, and can colonise heart valves (endocarditis), impair kidney function, and affect the liver. Small breed dogs are disproportionately affected by dental-origin endocarditis compared to large breeds — likely because the smaller cardiac anatomy tolerates bacterial seeding less well.

This systemic dimension is why veterinarians now treat dental disease as a whole-body health issue. A Yorkie with Stage 3–4 periodontal disease and elevated kidney values at a blood screen is not facing two coincidental problems — the dental disease is a plausible contributing cause of the kidney finding. Treating the teeth often stabilises the systemic picture.

What prevention actually changes

Daily brushing — the single most effective intervention

Plaque begins forming within hours of a meal and hardens into tartar — which cannot be removed by brushing — within 24–48 hours, according to VCA Animal Hospitals. This timeline is why daily brushing outperforms every other home care intervention. Brushing every other day means tartar forms on the days brushing is skipped. Three times a week means tartar is forming consistently between sessions.

Use a dog-specific enzymatic toothpaste. Enzymatic pastes contain glucose oxidase and lactoperoxidase — enzymes that continue working after the brush is put away, breaking down the bacterial film that forms plaque. Human toothpaste contains xylitol and fluoride, both toxic to dogs.

Professional cleanings under general anaesthesia

Home brushing maintains the gum line but cannot access sub-gingival plaque — the deposits below the gum line that drive Stage 2 and beyond. This is why the AVDC and most veterinary dental specialists recommend annual professional cleanings for toy breeds. For Yorkies with good home care, once-yearly cleaning is typical. For those with no home care or poor compliance history, every 6 months may be appropriate initially.

General anaesthesia is not optional for a complete dental cleaning — it is the standard of care for a reason. It allows full sub-gingival scaling, probing to assess attachment loss at every tooth, dental radiographs, and treatment of findings without causing distress. Anaesthesia-free dental cleaning (the "scraping" done at grooming salons) removes only visible supragingival tartar and provides no periodontal assessment. The AVDC has a formal position statement against anaesthesia-free dental procedures.

Dental adjuncts: chews, water additives, dental diets

Dental chews with the Veterinary Oral Health Council (VOHC) seal have demonstrated plaque or tartar reduction in controlled studies. They work as a mechanical supplement to brushing, not a replacement. Water additives with enzymatic activity provide some benefit. Prescription dental diets use a fibre matrix that creates abrasion as the tooth passes through the kibble. None of these reach the sub-gingival zone, so they are adjuncts to the primary interventions — brushing and professional cleaning — not substitutes.

Starting brushing: a realistic protocol

The most common reason Yorkie owners cite for not brushing is that the dog resists it. Resistance is almost always a training problem, not a physical impossibility — Yorkies that have been introduced to mouth handling from puppyhood accept brushing readily. For adult dogs with no prior training, a desensitisation approach works:

  1. Week 1: Touch the outside of the lips daily with a finger. No tools, no paste. Just acclimatisation. Give a treat immediately after.
  2. Week 2: Lift the lip and touch the outer surface of the teeth briefly with a finger. Treat every session.
  3. Week 3: Introduce a finger brush or soft puppy toothbrush with a small amount of enzymatic toothpaste. Brush the outer surfaces only — 10 seconds per side initially.
  4. Week 4+: Build to a full 30–60 second brush covering outer surfaces of all teeth. The inner surfaces are less critical — the tongue provides some mechanical cleaning there — but include them when the dog is comfortable.

The small jaw and narrow mouth of a Yorkie can make brushing the back molars awkward. A finger brush often provides better control in a small mouth than a handled toothbrush. The back premolars and molars are the highest-tartar sites — prioritise these over the front incisors, which get more natural cleaning from the tongue.

Dental health across the Yorkie lifespan

The trajectory of dental disease in Yorkies is largely determined by what happens before age 3. Dogs with established daily brushing routines and at least one professional cleaning by age 2 have a fundamentally different starting point than dogs that first present for a dental at age 5 with Stage 3 disease. This is why the conversations about dental care in broader health discussions matter — our guide to Yorkie weight and development by age covers the other health benchmarks that track alongside dental development across the lifespan.

In senior Yorkies (over 8 years), anaesthetic risk increases and needs to be weighed against dental benefit. Modern pre-anaesthetic bloodwork, intravenous fluid support during the procedure, and careful anaesthetic protocols have made senior dental procedures safe in otherwise healthy dogs — but the risk-benefit discussion becomes more individual. The argument for early prevention is partly that it prevents the difficult senior anaesthetic decision by keeping the mouth manageable before it becomes a crisis.

General coat and grooming condition in Yorkies is also a useful proxy for overall health status — dogs with deteriorating coat condition and increased matting sometimes have systemic issues, including dental-origin systemic disease, as a contributing factor. FAQ — Yorkshire Terrier dental disease

How common is dental disease in Yorkshire Terriers?

The AVMA estimates that 80% of dogs over 3 years have some form of periodontal disease. Yorkshire Terriers face elevated risk due to their small jaw size, tooth crowding, and tendency to retain deciduous teeth — all of which accelerate plaque and tartar formation compared to larger breeds.

Why are Yorkshire Terriers more prone to dental problems?

Their adult weight of 2–3.2 kg [AKC] means 42 adult teeth in one of the smallest jaws of any breed. Teeth are crowded, contact points trap more plaque, and the tongue has less room for natural cleaning. Retained baby teeth — common in toy breeds — add additional plaque traps. The anatomy creates a higher baseline plaque burden than the same care routine would produce in a larger breed.

What are the four stages of periodontal disease in dogs?

The AVDC defines: Stage 1 (gingivitis, reversible), Stage 2 (up to 25% attachment loss — irreversible but manageable), Stage 3 (25–50% attachment loss — some teeth saveable), Stage 4 (over 50% attachment loss — extractions required). Most dogs presenting for their first dental cleaning are already at Stage 2 or beyond.

Can dental disease affect a Yorkie's heart or kidneys?

Yes. The AVMA documents that bacteria from periodontal pockets enter the bloodstream and can affect the heart valves, kidneys, and liver. Small breeds with chronic untreated dental disease have documented associations with endocarditis and reduced kidney function. Dental disease is a systemic health issue, not only a cosmetic one.

How often should a Yorkie's teeth be brushed?

Daily brushing is the standard recommended by the AVMA and VCA Animal Hospitals. Plaque hardens into tartar within 24–48 hours, making every-other-day brushing significantly less protective than daily. Enzymatic toothpaste continues working after brushing — use a dog-specific formula. Human toothpaste contains xylitol and fluoride, which are toxic to dogs.

When should I start dental care for a Yorkie puppy?

Handle the mouth and introduce a soft finger brush from the first weeks after bringing the puppy home. Getting a puppy comfortable with mouth contact during the critical socialisation window (8–16 weeks) makes lifelong brushing far easier. Ask your vet to check for retained deciduous teeth at or before the 6-month check-up, typically addressed at the same time as spay or neuter surgery.

What is a retained deciduous tooth and why does it matter for Yorkies?

A retained deciduous tooth is a baby tooth that stays in place when the adult tooth erupts alongside it. VCA Animal Hospitals notes that toy breeds are particularly prone to this. The double row traps food and bacteria, accelerating tartar at that site and potentially causing the adult tooth to erupt at an abnormal angle. Retained deciduous teeth are typically extracted under anaesthesia at the time of spay or neuter.
